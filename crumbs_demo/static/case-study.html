Thesis

Crumbs can serve users and advertisers with the same privacy-safe signal

For users

Crumbs gives people transparency and control over their data, and lets them see products that genuinely interest them.

For advertisers

Crumbs helps advertisers reach and understand their audiences in environments where data is limited.

Together: users who trust Crumbs choose to share an aggregated signal, and that consented signal is what advertisers need when cookies and device IDs are unavailable. Each side's value depends on the other. The rest of this site covers how to build it: fix today's blocking, make router setup one click, start with measurement, and add targeting once the signal is trusted.

Strategy in one sentence

Diagnosis, guiding policy and coherent actions

Written to the test in Richard Rumelt's Good Strategy Bad Strategy: say what the problem is, make choices that rule things out, and take actions that support each other.

Crumbs can see which apps people use without knowing who they are, but today it costs publishers their auctions and has too few users to measure anything, so we will block tracking but not auctions, grow through routers rather than app downloads, measure app outcomes on phones first through the measurement companies advertisers already pay, and create audiences everywhere later, for web, CTV and router households. Privacy and accuracy are what make it different.

1 · Diagnosis

One unique asset, two blockers

  • The asset: a view of app activity at the network level, where other measurement has been weak since ATT.
  • Blocker one: Crumbs blocks auctions that publishers depend on.
  • Blocker two: too few users to report anything accurately.
2 · Guiding policy

Three choices

  • Block tracking, not auctions. Publishers become supporters instead of opponents.
  • Grow through routers and ISPs. Setup becomes one click instead of a manual DNS change.
  • Sell through measurement companies and curators. eyeo doesn't need its own advertiser sales team.
3 · Coherent actions

Each step makes the next one possible

  • Fix the blocking policy, and publishers support Crumbs.
  • Sign one router or ISP partner, and the panel grows large enough to measure.
  • Pilot measurement data with two measurement companies, and the first revenue arrives.
  • Use the same data to describe the audiences of apps and sites for curators.
From today

Two things need to change first

Measured today, Crumbs blocks whole independent auctions (Xandr, Ozone, Kargo and Magnite's server-side Prebid) while Google, the Meta Pixel and several ID providers pass straight through. Header-bidding value fell 32% to 100% on every site measured. The first step is to block identity rather than auctions. Publishers are the most affected group today, so we should also work with them to advocate for Crumbs. The second is distribution. Setting up Crumbs on a router is too hard for most people, and many ISP routers do not allow it, so router and ISP partnerships need to start now.

Target, then Scale
Why app first, when web is bigger

We start with app outcomes and extend to web later

Web outcome pools are several times the size of app marketing. We still start with apps for three reasons. The measurement gap is widest on iOS apps. DNS sees app traffic clearly. iCloud Private Relay hides much of Safari's traffic from any DNS resolver. Once app measurement is trusted, it can extend to household and cross-screen measurement, because a router can see every device in a home. The Where to start tab shows the split.

In scope

What we build

  • A published blocking policy: block identity, not commerce
  • A publisher coalition that advocates for Crumbs
  • Router and ISP partnerships for one-click setup
  • Opt-in, aggregate outcome measurement and fraud signals
  • Pre-registered lift readouts
  • Later, curation using inventory labels and opt-in interest groups of at least 100 people, with an Acceptable Ads quality bar
  • The trust stack: on-device processing, split aggregation, attestation
Out of scope

What we refuse

  • Individual user IDs, or segments smaller than 100 people, in the bidstream
  • Conquesting cohorts and anything built on sensitive categories
  • Selling data to funds or brokers
  • Building an advertiser sales team. Apart from a few reference lift studies, we sell through measurement companies and curators
  • Paying users cash for their data
  • Relying on people finding the app and changing their DNS settings
  • DNS-level ad replacement
  • Any product that makes protection depend on opting in
  • Blocking that decides which companies get paid rather than what data the user shares
Counterarguments, answered inline

Likely objections and our answers

Publishers will see Crumbs as a threat

Today's blocking does reduce their revenue. Once auctions are allowed again and Crumbs adds a privacy-safe signal, their readers become more valuable, which gives publishers a reason to support Crumbs.

Blocking looks like pay to play

Today some independent auctions are blocked while Google is not, and eyeo also receives Acceptable Ads payments. A single published policy that treats every company the same addresses this.

eyeo would be marking its own homework

We would publish the methodology, measure media eyeo did not curate, deliver results through MMPs and pre-register every lift test. These steps make the results checkable.

The panel skews toward privacy enthusiasts

Weight and calibrate against partners' real install counts, publish confidence intervals, and use ISP deals to broaden who is in the panel.

Measurement is a small market

It is the starting point. Curation and CTV are larger markets, and they depend on the signal being trusted first.

Apple could change the rules

This is the largest platform risk. We should avoid depending on any single private API and use routers to broaden the panel.

Live demo

Crumbs off and Crumbs on, side by side

The blocking findings came from this demo. Two browser windows load the same page, one through normal DNS and one through the Crumbs resolver, and the panel compares what each window requested and what Prebid bid.

The control panel below is running on this laptop. Its "Open A/B" buttons open the two windows on the right of the screen, so this page can stay on the left.

Why it isn't running here

The demo runs on a laptop, not in a web page

It starts two local proxies, a DNS filter and two Edge windows, and reads each window through the DevTools port. A hosted page cannot do any of that. When the demo is running, this same site is served from it at http://127.0.0.1:5380/case-study, and this tab shows the live control panel.

Step 1Two windows

Window A uses normal DNS. Window B uses the Crumbs resolver. Each is a fresh browser profile with its own proxy, so the browser cannot resolve names any other way.

Step 2Every request logged

The panel records each request with its outcome: allowed, blocked by Crumbs, blocked by the browser, or failed.

Step 3Prebid compared

Every two seconds the panel reads each window's Prebid results: bidders asked, bids received and the top bid per slot.

Step 4Test page

A test publisher page runs a real Prebid auction and also works from the Android emulator running the Crumbs app.

Demo script

Three steps, about five minutes

  1. TodayOpen A/B on the real sites with the fix switched off. Window B loses auctions to Crumbs' blocking.
  2. Proposed policyTick "Proposed policy: allow auctions" and reload. Window B's auctions come back while syncs and trackers stay blocked.
  3. Crumbs moduleTick "Crumbs Prebid module", pick a group and reload. Window B's bid requests now carry the group and no user IDs. Window A is unchanged.

The switches are in the strip under the control panel's header. The Prebid section of the panel shows what each window's bid request told bidders.

Our advocates

Publishers and curators will make the case for Crumbs

Both groups lose value when audience signals disappear, and both gain when a privacy-safe signal replaces them. Publishers need their readers to stay valuable. Curators need audience data they can package into deals and defend to buyers. Crumbs can give both what they need without tracking anyone, so both have a reason to promote it.

Publishers

  • Sites with no signal lose value as identifiers disappear, so a privacy-safe audience signal protects their revenue.
  • The block-identity policy restores auctions that Crumbs cuts today, such as Ozone's.
  • Curated deals pay publishers more: PubMatic reported eCPMs over 25% higher on curated deals than on non-curated deals.
  • They can promote the Crumbs opt-in to their own readers, which grows the panel.

Curators

  • Curation is the fastest-growing way programmatic media is bought, and it runs on audience data.
  • Opt-in interest groups and audience-composition labels give them a signal with a published privacy design, which they can show to cautious buyers.
  • The DNS route needs no code in the page, so a curator can work with an SSP to add Crumbs groups across many publishers at once.
  • The outcome panel lets them prove that their deals drove installs, which is what justifies their fees.

Lean into publishers

Why publishers can carry Crumbs' case for it

Who actually opposed Privacy Sandbox

The core objection to Privacy Sandbox came from ad tech companies. They were not against privacy. They were against one dominant platform changing the rules in a way that improved its own ads business while making it harder for everyone else to compete. That concern was serious enough for the UK's Competition and Markets Authority to investigate the changes and take commitments from Google.

What that means for Crumbs

Publishers did not need to be won over to privacy. They needed privacy that did not hand the advantage to one company. Crumbs can offer exactly that, as long as it does not repeat the pattern. Today it partly does: it blocks independent auctions while Google passes through, and eyeo also receives Acceptable Ads payments. Fixing that first turns publishers from the group Crumbs hurts into the group that vouches for it.

The rule that follows: Crumbs never changes what it blocks or allows in a way that favours eyeo's own business, or any one company's. The policy is published, applies to everyone the same way, and publishers help govern it.

A publisher programme

What we offer publishers, in the order we offer it

Each step gives publishers something before we ask for anything.

  1. Show them the gapA free paired report of their header-bidding value with Crumbs on and off, before and after the policy change. It opens the conversation with their own numbers.
  2. Restore their auctionsThe block-identity policy brings back auctions like Ozone's, the Guardian's top bidder in our runs, while ID syncs stay blocked.
  3. Ship Crumbs modeA Prebid module through Blockthrough that strips identifiers in the page and, later, carries the opt-in interest group.
  4. Share the upsidePublishers who promote the opt-in to readers get a share of the curation revenue earned on their inventory.
  5. Give them a voteA publisher seat on the blocking policy, modelled on the Acceptable Ads Committee, and co-published measurement.

Crumbs today · products

What Crumbs offers today

Crumbs is a DNS service from eyeo. It is available as an iOS app, an Android app and a DNS setting people can apply to their own router. Some details below still need confirming with the team.

Live

iOS and Android apps

"Crumbs: DNS Privacy" was released on the App Store on 11 August 2026. It blocks trackers at the network level across apps and websites, blocks malware and phishing sites, and shows which companies tried to track the user. crumbs.com also links to an Android app on Google Play.

Third-party estimate for iOS: about 350 installs and about 108 monthly active users. AppGoblin's figures are modelled, so they need checking against eyeo's own data. We have no estimate for Android.

Live, self-configured

Router DNS setting

Users can point their own router at Crumbs' DNS servers through the Crumbs website. Modern routers that support it can use encrypted DNS (DoH), which the site describes as extra privacy for modern routers only.

This covers every device on the home network, including TVs and consoles, but only for people who change their router settings themselves.

To confirm

Router and ISP partnerships

The CTO has said eyeo is working with routers. It is not yet clear whether that means router makers, ISPs or improvements to the self-configured setup, or how far along it is.

This matters most for the plan, because partnerships are the only route to a household panel at scale.

To confirm

Resolver infrastructure

Crumbs answers lookups from its own DNS servers, and eyeo sets the blocking policy. crumbs.com states ISO 27001:2022 certification.

Who operates the resolver, and how it logs, affects every privacy claim and any move to attested hardware. This is a week-one question.

What this means for the plan

The panel is very small today

If the third-party estimate is close, the iOS user base is in the hundreds, not the millions, and Android is unlikely to change that by an order of magnitude. The outcome panel can be piloted on a small base, but it cannot be sold at scale until the opt-in base is much larger. Router and ISP partnerships therefore start on day one, alongside the first measurement pilot. Router and ISP partnerships matter most, because self-setup is too hard for most users. Publishers promoting Crumbs to their readers can also help. Fixing the blocking policy comes first of all, because it affects how publishers and ad tech partners see Crumbs.

The user estimate covers iOS only. eyeo's own figures for iOS, Android and router users are the first thing to confirm.

Router setup

Most users will not be able to set up Crumbs on their router themselves

Setting up Crumbs on a router today means opening the router's admin page, finding the DNS setting and typing in Crumbs' server addresses. Many people will not do this. On many ISP-supplied routers it is not possible at all.

1
Open the ISP router's admin pageThe router in this test was a Sky hub, which shows the connection, Wi-Fi and connected devices.
2
Look for the DNS settingGateway, then Connection, then Local IP Network shows DHCP and IPv6 settings, but no DNS field.
3
The setting does not existSky hubs do not let users choose their own DNS server, according to Sky's community forum.
4
The only workaround is a second routerA mesh system such as TP-Link Deco, run in router mode, can set its own DNS. This creates double NAT and is beyond most users.

This was one test on one household's equipment. Other ISP routers may behave differently, and it is worth checking BT, Virgin Media and TalkTalk hubs in the first 30 days.

Day one priority

Router and ISP partnerships should make Crumbs a one-click setup

The household panel, CTV measurement and any scale in the ad products all depend on routers. Self-setup will not reach enough homes, so partnerships need to start immediately rather than in a later phase.

ISPs

An ISP can switch Crumbs on for millions of households from its own systems. In the UK, BT Group has about 8.8 million broadband customers, Sky about 6.7 million and Virgin Media O2 about 5.7 million.

Route: ship Crumbs as a security app on the ISP's gateway. The prpl standard lets ISPs install third-party apps on routers without a firmware update. AT&T says 12 million of its gateways support it, and Vodafone UK's Ultra Hub 7 uses it.

Router and mesh makers

People who buy their own router or mesh system can turn Crumbs on in the maker's app with one tap. In the US, eero, TP-Link and Netgear each account for about 10% of routers in Ookla's speed-test data.

Route: a Crumbs option inside the eero, TP-Link Deco, Netgear or Asus app. This also reaches households whose ISP router is locked, because the mesh system becomes the router.

ISP gateways are mostly built by manufacturers such as Arcadyan, Askey, Sagemcom, Calix and Arris, but the ISP decides what runs on them. For ISP routers, the ISP is the partner to approach.

Crumbs usually blocks a company's ID-sync subdomain and leaves its bidding subdomain alone. The exceptions block the auction itself, and those are what cost revenue.

CompanyBlocked by CrumbsAllowedWhat is cut

Also blocked, logged by company only: IAS, ID5, EUID, Eyeota, LinkedIn ads, and parts of Quantcast and Taboola. Adobe's typekit.net web fonts are blocked too, which is plain breakage.

Right

Blocking identity

  • ID syncs for The Trade Desk, Magnite, Index, Criteo, TripleLift, PubMatic and Amazon
  • Data brokers: Lotame, LiveRamp, ID5, EUID
  • This matches Crumbs' goal and costs little revenue
Wrong

Blocking commerce

  • Whole auctions: Xandr, Ozone, Kargo, Magnite's server-side Prebid. Their requests carry the same kinds of identifiers as auctions Crumbs allows, so blocking them decides who gets paid, not what the user leaks.
  • Ozone was the Guardian's top bidder at $3.00, and is a UK publisher-owned platform
  • Verification (IAS) and Comscore measurement lower inventory value without stopping cross-site profiling
Missed

Identity still flows

  • The Meta Pixel shares Facebook's domain, so DNS cannot block it without breaking Facebook
  • Google Analytics and Tag Manager
  • IDs already stored by the site travel inside bid requests: Magnite's carried a SharedID, and Google Ad Manager's carried SharedID, ID5, Criteo, Lotame and Permutive IDs
  • Wunderkind's device-ID call

A Crumbs user's identity still mostly reaches Google, while some independent auctions are blocked. This does not fit eyeo's aim of an open web that is private by design. Because eyeo also receives Acceptable Ads payments, it could also be criticised as pay to play.

A tension for the plan

Crumbs' own homepage lists MMPs among the trackers it blocks

crumbs.com shows AppsFlyer, Branch, Kochava and Tenjin as blocked, described as tracing app installs, alongside Microsoft's Xandr auction described as putting the user up for auction. Selling aggregated outcome data to MMPs is still possible, because Crumbs would be selling its own privacy-protected data rather than letting MMPs track users. It does need a clear explanation for users and for the MMPs themselves.

Goal 2: ads without trackers, but personalised

Crumbs does not yet add a privacy-safe signal

Nothing from Crumbs reaches a bid request today. Buyers see a less identifiable user, so there are fewer bids at lower prices. Crumbs currently improves privacy by removing data, without offering a replacement signal.

One route needs no code in the page. DNS cannot change what is inside a request, but the resolver chooses which IP address the request is sent to. If a partner SSP reserves a block of addresses, Crumbs can answer each opted-in device with the address that stands for its audience group. The SSP reads the address its server was reached on and adds the group to the bid request as audience data. This is what the CTO has suggested, and it is covered on the How it works in Prebid tab.

Our biggest advocates

Publishers are the most affected today and could be our strongest supporters

Why publishers would support Crumbs

  • Once auctions pass and identity is blocked, header-bidding value should return close to the Crumbs-off level, with the same privacy for readers.
  • A privacy-safe audience signal, such as an opt-in interest group or an audience-composition label, raises the value of their readers.
  • Sites with no signal at all lose value as identifiers disappear. Crumbs offers a way to keep audience value without tracking, so publishers benefit when their readers install it and opt in.
  • Publisher-owned platforms like Ozone are blocked today. Restoring their auctions removes a reason for publishers to oppose Crumbs.

How we would work with them

  • Co-publish the paired Crumbs-on and Crumbs-off measurement with a first group of UK publishers.
  • Ship a "Crumbs mode" in their Prebid setup through Blockthrough, which already runs in publishers' ad code.
  • Publishers promote the Crumbs opt-in to their readers as part of their own privacy story.
  • Give publishers a seat on the policy, in the way the Acceptable Ads Committee works today.
What a version built for the goals looks like

Remove tracking and add a privacy-safe signal

  1. Block identity, not commerceAllow auctions and ad serving. Block syncs, data brokers and cross-site analytics. Allow privacy-safe verification and measurement. One policy for everyone, published like the Acceptable Ads Standard.
  2. Steer instead of blockSend ib.adnxs.com to Xandr's existing cookieless endpoint, and do the same for any partner with a no-ID endpoint. This uses the same kind of influence as Acceptable Ads.
  3. Close the gaps in the pageA Crumbs mode in Prebid through Blockthrough, and eyeo's browser SDK stripping identifiers and the Meta Pixel. DNS cannot reach inside a request.
  4. Add the personalised halfAn opt-in interest-group ID for groups of 100 or more people, passed in Prebid bid requests as audience data, with curated deals so buyers can target it. Crumbs measurement replaces the blocked pixels.
  5. Prove every changePaired measurement with the target of syncs blocked, no identifiers in bid requests, and header-bidding value back near the Crumbs-off level.

    Worked example · DNS steering

    How an IP address becomes audience data in the bid request

    A DNS answer contains only an address or a refusal. It cannot carry key-values. The group is carried by which address Crumbs returns, and only the SSP knows how to read it.

    Set-up: the SSP reserves one address per group for its bidding hostname
    Address for bid.ssp.exampleMeaning to the SSP
    2001:db8:ab::1Default: no group
    2001:db8:ab::7Group 7: personal finance
    2001:db8:ab::9Group 9: travel

    Addresses are examples from the documentation range. IPv6 makes reserving thousands of addresses cheap.

    What the resolver can and cannot return
    • An address, such as bid.ssp.example is at 2001:db8:ab::7.
    • A refusal, such as "no such domain". This is how Crumbs blocks trackers today.
    • Nothing else. There is no field for data, so nothing reaches the page or Prebid as a key-value.
    1. Prebid needs the SSPPrebid.js is about to send a bid request to bid.ssp.example, so the phone asks where that host is.
    2. Crumbs answers with the group's addressThis user opted in and chose personal finance, so the answer is 2001:db8:ab::7. Everyone else gets ::1. To the browser it is a normal address.
    3. The request is unchangedThe browser sends Prebid's normal bid request to ::7. The certificate is for the hostname, so it is valid on every address.
    4. The SSP decodes the addressIts server notes it was reached on ::7, looks up group 7, and writes user.data segment 534 into the OpenRTB request. A curator rule can attach deal crumbs-fintech-installers.
    5. The deal comes back as a key-valueDSPs bid on the group or deal. If that bid wins, its deal ID returns in the bid response, and Prebid passes it to the ad server as hb_deal, alongside hb_pb and hb_bidder.
    Step 4: what the SSP adds before calling DSPs
    "user": {
  "data": [{
    "name": "crumbs.com",
    "ext": { "segtax": 4 },
    "segment": [{ "id": "534" }]
  }]
},
"imp": [{ "pmp": { "deals": [
  { "id": "crumbs-fintech-installers" }
]}}]
    Step 5: key-values Prebid sends to the ad server
    hb_bidder = "ssp"
hb_pb     = "2.40"
hb_deal   = "crumbs-fintech-installers"
hb_size   = "300x250"

    In this server-side version, the group itself never becomes an ad server key-value. Only the deal ID of a winning bid does, which lets the publisher prioritise Crumbs deals in its ad server.

    Who has to know the group. Step 2 only works if whatever answers the lookup knows the user's group. The best place is the phone: if the Crumbs app handles DNS on the device, it swaps in the group's address itself, and the group never reaches Crumbs' servers. If lookups go straight to Crumbs' resolver, Crumbs would have to hold each device's group, which weakens the privacy design. On a router, the group would be a household group held by the router. How the iOS app handles DNS today is a week-one question.

    Crumbs mode

    A Prebid configuration that removes identity and adds one group

    The production configuration. It uses Prebid's existing activity controls and real-time data module system. The prototype above shows the same idea working in the demo.

    pbjs.setConfig({
  // Prebid activity controls: switch off identity
  allowActivities: {
    syncUser:     { default: false },  // no cookie syncs
    transmitEids: { default: false },  // no extended IDs to bidders
    accessDevice: { default: false }   // ID modules cannot store IDs
  },
  // A Crumbs real-time data module adds the group
  realTimeData: {
    auctionDelay: 50,
    dataProviders: [{
      name: "crumbs",
      waitForIt: true,
      params: {
        minGroupSize: 100,     // never send a smaller group
        dropOtherUserData: true
      }
    }]
  }
});
    • Activity controls are Prebid's built-in privacy switches. They stop user syncs, stop extended IDs reaching bidders, and stop ID modules writing to storage, without touching the auction.
    • A real-time data module is how data providers add first-party data to bid requests before the auction. A Crumbs module would add the interest group to user.data.
    • Distribution comes through Blockthrough, part of eyeo, which already runs in many publishers' ad code, so publishers do not need a new vendor.
    • Prebid Server supports the same activity controls in account settings, so server-side auctions and in-app auctions through Prebid Mobile can follow the same rules.
    • Open question: which route to take to production for learning the group. The prototype uses the DNS answer. Other candidates are eyeo's browser components and an SDK in partner apps.

    Pay per ad

    A percentage of media, or a CPM?

    Percentage of media

    • The fee grows with the value of the deal, so eyeo shares in better prices.
    • Curators already charge this way. The median curation fee is 14% of media [1], so a 4% data fee fits inside it.
    • Easier to explain to publishers as a share of their own upside.

    Fixed CPM

    • Predictable for the buyer, and a familiar way to price audience data.
    • Protects eyeo when inventory clears cheaply.
    • Can look expensive on low-price inventory and push buyers back to the open auction.

    Start with a percentage of media, with a small CPM floor so cheap inventory still pays its way. Test both with the first curator.

    A question for the team

    Are we an ads platform?

    Pay per ad does not require eyeo to build one. Curators and SSPs already run the deals and collect the money, so eyeo can sell its signal into them as a data provider. But there are three ways to play it, and each changes what eyeo builds and who it competes with.

    OptionWhat eyeo buildsHow eyeo earnsThe trade-off
    1 · Data providerThe interest groups, audience labels and a list partners can use. No buying or selling of ads.A data fee on media in partners' dealsSmallest build and stays neutral. Smaller share, and depends on partners promoting Crumbs data.
    2 · CuratorA curation desk that packages Crumbs deals across several SSPs, using eyeo's publisher relationships through Acceptable Ads and Blockthrough.A curation margin, typically larger than a data fee [1]More revenue and more control of quality.     3 · Ads platformAn exchange or ad network that runs auctions and serves ads.A take rate on all mediaLargest prize, but a large build, and eyeo would be blocking some platforms while running its own. That is the pay-to-play pattern this plan is trying to fix.

    My starting view: begin as a data provider, earn the trust of publishers and curators, and only consider curating once Crumbs deals have proven results. I would not build an exchange. But this is a real choice for eyeo, and I would like the team's view.

    Who Crumbs pays

    The people and networks the data comes from get paid

    Crumbs works like a measurement panel. Others pay for the same thing: Nielsen gives panel members up to $60 a year [2], Brave gives users 70% of its ad revenue [3], and Google gives Apple 36% of its Safari search revenue [4].

    Routers and ISPs are paid in cash. They get 30% of the revenue from the households they bring. Users are paid in kind, with free protection and control. Cash doesn't work: eyeo would break even at about $7 per user a year.

    Paying for data has rules. In the EU, an ISP needs its own consent from each subscriber [5]. In California, any cash for data needs a published estimate of what the data is worth [6].

    Zero trust

    No one, including eyeo, should be able to see what an individual does.

    This is the design goal. Each layer below supports it, and each can be checked independently.

    How it fits together

    The DNS path, split so no single operator holds both who and what

    The end state, reached in phases: the attested resolver first, then the independent relay.

    1. Encrypt on the phoneThe lookup is encrypted so only the resolver can read it, then sent through the relay. This is Oblivious DNS over HTTPS.
    2. Relay strips the IPThe relay forwards the sealed query without the user's IP address. It sees who is asking, but not what.
    3. Resolver answers blindThe resolver reads the query and applies the published policy, but never sees the IP. Cache misses go upstream batched and padded, to blunt timing analysis.
    4. Everyone checks the codeThe phone checks the resolver's attestation before its first lookup. Relay and resolver attest to each other, against images in a public log.

    What attestation proves

    The running code matches the published code

    • The relay strips IP addresses and keeps no logs
    • The resolver accepts queries only from attested relays and keeps no logs
    • Relay and resolver verify each other before talking
    • Every deployed image is in a public transparency log, so a quiet change is visible
    What it cannot prove

    Risks outside the secure hardware

    • Network timing: an upstream cache miss leaves the enclave in plain DNS, so timing can link IP to domain
    • The hardware root: AMD, Intel or the cloud provider joins the trust base, and TEEs have a side-channel history
    • Key control: one operator holding both deployment keys can attempt a coordinated change
    • Mitigations: big shared caches, batched and delayed upstream queries, padding, independent operators
    Collusion resistance

    What a breach would need

    Same operator, no TEEsOne insider
    Same operator, relay and resolver in TEEsOne hardware break, or network timing analysis
    Independent relay operator, no TEEsTwo companies colluding
    Independent relay operator, both in TEEs, mutual attestation, transparency logTwo companies colluding, plus hardware breaks, plus timing analysis

    The same pattern Apple uses for Private Relay, with independent partners running the second hop.

    Phasing and cost

    The first phase does not need the full infrastructure

    Engineering and audits dominate the cost, not hardware. Plausibly low single-digit $M a year at scale assumption, against year-3 obtainable revenue of $18–63M.

    First 30 days

    Five measurements will decide our approach

    Everything in the first month is aimed at these, using lab devices and synthetic data. No real users are touched.

    Number 1Publisher revenue gap

    Header-bidding value with Crumbs on and off, before and after the policy change.

    Number 2User base and router reach

    Real iOS and router users, and how many households a partner could switch on. This sets how much can be sold and how much growth is needed.

    Number 3Fingerprint coverage

    Share of the top 200 iOS apps whose first opens we can identify cleanly.

    Number 4Reportable apps and countries

    How many app and country combinations have enough opted-in users to report accurate numbers after privacy noise is added.

    Number 5Buyer interest

    Whether two MMPs see a calibration feed as worth paying for.

    Week 1

    Establish the facts

    • Repeat the paired Crumbs-on and Crumbs-off measurement across 30 publisher sites with more page views, logging hostnames
    • User and opt-in counts by market, and the status of the router work
    • Router setup test on the main UK ISP hubs: BT, Sky, Virgin Media, TalkTalk
    • Visibility audit on test devices: Safari relay, Firefox, apps with their own DoH
    • Draft the one-page published design
    Week 2

    Build

    • Draft the block-identity policy and test sending ib.adnxs.com to Xandr's cookieless endpoint
    • Fingerprint lab: top 200 iOS apps through install, open, background and idle
    • Privacy and accuracy simulator on synthetic data sized to the real base
    • Confirm who operates the resolver, and its logging, retention and confidential computing options
    Week 3

    Test demand

    • Two publisher conversations, starting with UK news publishers, about advocating for Crumbs
    • Router conversations with one mesh maker, such as eero or TP-Link, and one UK ISP, about one-click setup
    • Two MMP conversations, one incrementality vendor, one SSP curator
    • Inventory propensity test against a contextual baseline
    • Red-team the design: differencing, probing, collusion
    Week 4

    Decide

    • Memo on which approach to take, based on the five measurements
    • Publish the policy draft with publisher feedback
    • Pilot terms with one MMP
    • Opt-in experiment plan to test value-exchange variants
